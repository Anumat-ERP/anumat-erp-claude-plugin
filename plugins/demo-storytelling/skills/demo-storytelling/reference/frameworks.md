# Storytelling frameworks

Nine frameworks. Pick **one** as the spine of the demo. Mixing two usually
means neither lands. The one-sentence pitch and the one memorable number are
the exceptions: they sit inside whichever spine you choose.

The worked examples use an invented product, **Shelfie**, a stock-tracking app
for small cafés, so the structure is visible without any real product in the
way. Replace it with yours.

## Choosing

| Situation | Use |
|---|---|
| Hackathon final, 3 minutes, live product | Show, don't tell, on a Problem → Solution → Proof → Ask spine |
| Investor pitch, 5 to 10 minutes, slides plus demo | Problem → Solution → Proof → Ask |
| Executive or steering audience, wants the point first | SCQA |
| Audience that doesn't know the domain | Pick-a-persona, or the Pixar story spine |
| Customer demo, the buyer is the user | Hero's journey (StoryBrand) |
| Sprint review, showing a change | Before/After/Bridge |
| The result is a measurable gain | Data storytelling, inside any spine |
| Hallway, lift, the first line of any pitch | One-sentence pitch |

---

## 1. Problem → Solution → Proof → Ask

**When:** the default for pitches and hackathon finals. Judges and investors
are scoring against exactly these four questions, so answer them in their
order.

**Shape:**
- **Problem**: who hurts, how much, how often. One concrete case, not a market.
- **Solution**: what you built, in one sentence, then shown.
- **Proof**: evidence it works. A live run, a pilot number, a quote, a metric.
- **Ask**: what you want from this room, specifically.

**Mini-example (Shelfie):**
- Problem: "Mia runs a café. Every Friday she finds out she's out of oat milk
  when a customer orders a flat white."
- Solution: "Shelfie counts stock from one photo of the shelf and orders
  before she runs out."
- Proof: "Three cafés, six weeks, zero stock-outs on tracked items."
- Ask: "We want two more pilot cafés in this city. If you own one, find us
  after."

## 2. SCQA (Minto pyramid)

**When:** senior or time-poor audiences who want the answer before the
reasoning. Also the structure for a written summary or the first slide.

**Shape:** **Situation** (what everyone agrees on) → **Complication** (what
changed or went wrong) → **Question** (the one it raises) → **Answer** (your
product, stated as the answer to that question). Then the rest of the talk
supports the answer.

**Mini-example:**
- S: "Small cafés order stock by eye, once a week."
- C: "Oat milk sales doubled in two years, and it runs out mid-week."
- Q: "How does a café with no back office know what to order before it runs
  out?"
- A: "One photo a day. Shelfie does the counting and the ordering."

## 3. Pixar story spine

**When:** the audience doesn't know the domain and you need them to care in
under a minute. Good openers for non-technical rooms.

**Shape:** *Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of that… Because of
that… Until finally… And ever since then…*

**Mini-example:** "Once upon a time there was a café owner called Mia. Every
day she checked the fridge by eye. One day she ran out of oat milk during the
morning rush. Because of that, she lost twelve orders. Because of that, she
started over-ordering and throwing milk away. Until finally she took one photo
of her shelf with Shelfie. And ever since then, the order goes in before she
runs out."

Speak it without the scaffolding words once it is written. "Once upon a time"
is for drafting, not for the stage.

## 4. Hero's journey, StoryBrand-style

**When:** customer demos and sales. The buyer must see themselves as the one
who wins. The product is the guide, never the hero. A demo where the product
is the hero reads as bragging.

**Shape (Donald Miller's StoryBrand compression):** a **character** (the user)
wants something → has a **problem** (external, internal, and "it shouldn't be
this way") → meets a **guide** (your product) with empathy and authority →
who gives them a **plan** (three steps at most) → and **calls them to act** →
which ends in **success**, and avoids **failure** that you name.

**Mini-example:**
- Character: Mia, café owner, wants to spend mornings with customers.
- Problem: external, stock runs out; internal, she feels disorganised; "it
  shouldn't take an hour of counting to buy milk".
- Guide: "We've counted shelves in forty cafés. It's always the milk."
- Plan: photo, check, approve.
- Call to act: "Take your first photo tomorrow."
- Success: Friday rush with a full fridge. Failure avoided: turning customers
  away.

## 5. Before / After / Bridge

**When:** sprint reviews, feature launches, anything where the change is the
point. Also good for a 1-minute slot.

**Shape:** **Before**: the world today, felt. **After**: the world with the
change, felt. **Bridge**: how you get from one to the other, which is the live
demo.

**Mini-example:**
- Before: "Friday, 7 a.m. Mia is on her knees counting cartons."
- After: "Friday, 7 a.m. Mia is serving. The order went in on Wednesday."
- Bridge: "Here's what she did on Wednesday." (demo: one photo, one approve)

## 6. Show, don't tell: the demo as the story

**When:** any live demo. This is less a spine than a rule for filling one:
every claim is shown on screen instead of stated.

**Shape:** replace each adjective with a click. Not "it's fast" but a timer
visible while it runs. Not "it's easy" but the persona doing it in two taps.
The Say line narrates what the persona is doing and why it matters; the screen
provides the evidence.

**Mini-example:** instead of "Shelfie recognises products automatically", say
"Mia takes one photo", tap the shutter, and let the counted list appear. Pause.
Let the room read it. Then: "Twelve products, counted."

## 7. One-sentence pitch (elevator formula)

**When:** the first or second line of every demo, the answer to "so what is
it?", and the hallway after.

**Formula:** *For* [target user] *who* [need or pain], [product] *is a*
[category] *that* [key benefit]. *Unlike* [current alternative], *it*
[differentiator].

**Mini-example:** "For café owners who run out of stock mid-week, Shelfie is a
stock tracker that orders from one photo of the shelf. Unlike a spreadsheet,
it counts for you."

Then cut it for speech: "Shelfie counts a café's stock from one photo and
orders before you run out." One breath. If it takes two, it is too long.

## 8. Pick-a-persona: a day in the life

**When:** the product has several steps or several users, and a feature tour
would lose the room. A named person gives the audience someone to follow.

**Shape:** one named user, one day, told in time stamps. Each moment in the
day is one step of the demo. Other users enter only when the persona's day
needs them.

**Mini-example:** "7:00, Mia opens. 7:05, one photo of the fridge. 7:06,
Shelfie says oat milk runs out Thursday. 7:07, she approves the order. 11:00,
the supplier confirms. Mia never opened a spreadsheet."

Use the same name every time. "Mia" beats "the user" every time.

## 9. Data storytelling: one number people remember

**When:** there is a measurable result. Put it inside whichever spine you
chose, at the Proof moment.

**Shape:** one number, made concrete, compared to something the audience
already understands. Say it, pause, say what it means.

- Pick **one**. Three numbers are remembered as none.
- Make it human-scaled: "an hour every Friday" beats "4.3% efficiency".
- Compare it: "that's a full shift a month".
- Show it large, alone on screen, for as long as it takes to say.

**Mini-example:** "Our pilot cafés got back **52 minutes a week**. [pause]
That's one extra hour of serving customers, every week, for every café."

Be honest about the source: "in a six-week pilot with three cafés" is
stronger than an unqualified claim a judge will pull apart in Q&A.
