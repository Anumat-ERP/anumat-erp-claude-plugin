# How to speak

Delivery is a skill with specific, practisable parts. None of them need
natural talent. Warm up first with
`${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/skills/demo-storytelling/reference/warmup.md`.

## Open without "Hi, I'm…"

The first ten seconds are when the room decides whether to listen. Your name
is on the programme. Start with the story, and introduce yourselves after the
hook or at the end.

Openers that work:

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| A moment | "Friday, 7 a.m. Mia opens her café and the oat milk is gone." |
| A question with a show of hands | "Who here has ever waited three days for someone to approve an expense?" |
| A number | "Small cafés throw away one litre of milk in five." |
| A contrast | "It takes Mia an hour to count her stock. It takes Shelfie one photo." |
| A promise | "In three minutes you'll watch a café order a week of stock without counting a thing." |

Memorise the first two lines word for word. Everything after can flex; the
opening must not.

## Signposting

Tell the room where they are, so they can follow while you click.

- **Map at the start:** "Three things: the problem, Mia's morning, and what
  we need from you."
- **Transitions:** "That's the problem. Here's what Mia does instead."
- **Before the wow:** "Watch the number on the right."
- **Before the ask:** "So here's what we're asking for."

A signpost is one short sentence. It costs two seconds and saves the room
from getting lost.

## Pause after key lines

A pause tells the audience "that mattered". Beginners fill every gap;
experienced speakers leave them on purpose.

- Pause **two seconds** after the pain, after the wow, after the number, and
  after the ask.
- Mark them in the script with `[pause]`.
- A pause feels three times longer to the speaker than to the audience. Count
  "one-thousand-one, one-thousand-two" in your head.

## Pace

- Target 130 to 150 words per minute; see
  `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/skills/demo-storytelling/reference/time-budgets.md`.
- Adrenaline speeds you up by 20% or more. If rehearsal pace is right, stage
  pace will be fast. Rehearse slightly slow.
- Slow down for names, numbers and the one-sentence pitch. Speed up only for
  asides.

## Volume and projection

- Speak to the back row, not to the first. If there is a microphone, speak at
  normal volume 3 to 5 cm from it and keep it there when you turn your head.
- Project from the belly, not the throat: breathe low, and let the sound ride
  on the out-breath.
- Finish sentences at full volume. Nervous speakers fade on the last words,
  which are usually the important ones.
- Pitch goes down at the end of a statement. Rising at the end turns claims
  into questions.

## Filler words

"Um", "so", "like", "basically" and "actually" are signs of thinking aloud.

- Replace the filler with a pause. Silence sounds confident; "um" doesn't.
- Record a rehearsal and count fillers per minute. Under two per minute is
  fine.
- Start sentences with the noun, not "So…".

## Eye contact in 3-second chunks

- Look at one person for about three seconds, or one full phrase. Then move to
  someone in a different part of the room.
- Cover the room in a loose Z: back left, front right, back right, front left.
- Look at the judges more than anyone else, but not only at them.
- When the screen is the point, look at it together with the audience, then
  turn back to them to say what it means.

## Hands

- Open hands, palms visible, between waist and shoulders. It reads as honest.
- Point at the screen with an open hand, not a finger, and only at what
  matters.
- No pockets, no crossed arms, no fidgeting with the clicker. If hands shake,
  rest one lightly on the lectern or hold a card.

## Where to stand

- Stand on the **side of the screen the audience reads first** (their left in
  left-to-right languages), so their eyes travel from you to the content.
- Never stand in the projector beam or between the audience and the screen.
- If one person drives and one speaks: the driver sits or stands at the
  laptop, out of the light; the speaker stands near the screen and owns the
  room.
- Face the audience. Talk to the screen and you lose the room.

## Talking while clicking, versus clicking then talking

Two valid modes. Choose per step and write it in the script.

| Mode | Use when | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Talk while clicking** | Simple, fast steps; a separate driver | The speaker narrates intent; the driver clicks on cue |
| **Click, then talk** | The step is the wow, or the result needs reading | Do it in silence. Pause. Then say what happened and why it matters |

Solo presenters: say the sentence, then click. Your brain does one thing at a
time under stress. Never read the UI aloud ("and now I click Settings").

## When the laptop freezes

Say one calm line, then act. See
`${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/skills/demo-storytelling/reference/recovery.md` for the
full ladder.

- "While that loads, here's why it matters…" and keep telling the story.
- Give it about 10 seconds. Then switch to the backup video without apology.
- Never debug on stage. Never say "it worked five minutes ago".

## End on the ask

- The last line is the ask, said slowly, then `[pause]`, then "Thank you".
- Make the ask specific: an amount, a pilot, an introduction, a vote.
- Loop back to the persona if you can: "Mia got her Fridays back. Help us give
  that to a thousand more cafés."
- Don't trail off with "so, yeah, that's it". Stop on purpose.

## Nerves

Nerves are normal and mostly invisible. The audience wants you to do well.

- The racing heart is the same arousal as excitement. Research on reappraisal
  (Brooks, 2014) found that saying "I am excited" beat trying to calm down.
- The warm-up breathing does more than willpower. Do the physiological sigh
  just before you walk on.
- Know the first two lines cold. Once they are out, the rest follows.
- Hold something: a clicker or a card gives the hands a job.
