# Finding people to talk to

The goal of the first round is 30-50 real conversations' worth of names, in
one segment. Most will not reply. That is normal.

## The warm intro ladder

Work from the warmest route down. Each step down lowers the reply rate, so use
the upper steps first.

1. **People you know who have the problem.** Useful, but discount their
   kindness; they are likely to be polite.
2. **Direct intros from people you know.** "Who do you know who runs a clinic
   and hates their scheduling?" A specific ask gets specific names.
3. **Second-degree intros** through advisors, investors, former colleagues,
   university networks, and suppliers who serve the segment.
4. **Communities where the segment already gathers** (below).
5. **Cold outreach** by email or direct message.

## Where the segment gathers

- **Online communities:** forums, subreddits, Slack and Discord servers,
  Telegram and Facebook groups, LinkedIn groups. Read for a week before
  posting. Follow the rules; many ban promotion, and they are right to.
- **Industry associations and events:** trade associations, conferences,
  training days, and supplier events. Organisers and speakers are good intro
  hubs.
- **Communities around existing tools:** user groups, forums and support
  communities of the software they already use, and review sites where people
  describe what they dislike. A one-star review with a detailed complaint is a
  person with a problem and an opinion.
- **Meetups and in-person groups** in the relevant field.
- **Job posts:** a company hiring for a role that exists only because of the
  problem is a company with the problem.

## Example: a small local market

In a small market, where most businesses are owner-run and relationships matter
more than email, in-person routes often outperform online ones:

- visit shops or offices at quiet hours and ask for ten minutes, with a clear
  "I am not selling anything";
- go through local business associations, chambers of commerce, and sector
  groups, which often run events and newsletters;
- ask accountants, suppliers, and distributors who serve many businesses in
  the segment for introductions;
- use the messaging apps the segment actually uses, which may not be email;
- speak the local language and bring a short printed note explaining who you
  are.

Adjust to your own market. The principle is to go where trust already exists.

## Honest outreach

Cold messages work when they are short, specific, and ask for learning rather
than a sale. Rules:

- say who you are and why you picked them;
- say you are early and are not selling anything today, if that is true;
- ask for a specific, small amount of time;
- make it easy to say no;
- no fake urgency, no fake scarcity, no pretending a product exists.

Follow up once, a few days later. After two unanswered messages, stop.

## Compound the list: always ask for two more

End every interview with: "Who else should I talk to about this? Could you
introduce me to two people?" A good interview turns into two warmer leads. If
nobody ever offers an intro, that is itself a weak signal about the pain.

## How many interviews is enough?

There is no fixed number. A common heuristic is that patterns start to repeat
after roughly **5-10 interviews per segment**; this is sometimes called
saturation. Signs you have reached it:

- the last three interviews added no new pain or workaround;
- you can predict answers before they come;
- your synthesis board stops changing.

Signs you have not:

- every interview tells a different story (your segment is probably too wide;
  split it);
- you are only hearing from one kind of person (friends, one company, one
  channel).

Treat the heuristic as a checkpoint, not a finish line. Keep talking to
customers after you stop doing formal discovery.

## Tracking

Keep a simple list: name, segment, channel, date contacted, reply, booked,
interviewed, intros given. Reply rate by channel tells you something about the
**channel hypothesis** too: if nobody in the segment answers anywhere, reaching
them later as customers will also be hard.
